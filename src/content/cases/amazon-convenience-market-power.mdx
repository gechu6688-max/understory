## Problem

Amazon is often associated with low prices, fast delivery, and convenience. For a consumer, those benefits are real: buying from Amazon can save time, reduce search costs, and make price comparison easier.

The harder question is whether those benefits are automatically good for consumers in the long run. If convenience helps Amazon become harder to avoid, and if rivals become weaker, then the same system that feels consumer-friendly today could create more pricing power later.

This case is a draft. Several claims still need stronger source verification before publication.

## Context

Amazon is not only an online store. It also operates a marketplace for third-party sellers, logistics and fulfillment services, advertising services, subscriptions, and cloud computing. That makes the consumer experience simple on the surface, but the business system behind it is complex.

The FTC and a group of states filed an antitrust case against Amazon in 2023. Their complaint argues that Amazon uses exclusionary conduct to maintain monopoly power in online superstore and marketplace-service markets. This draft treats those claims as allegations, not proven findings.

  The FTC complaint and press release are useful evidence of a regulatory theory of harm. They do not by themselves prove that Amazon violated the law.

## Evidence

Current verified evidence in this draft supports three limited points:

- Amazon's own public filing describes a large, multi-part business facing intense competition across retail, subscriptions, advertising, and technology services.
- The FTC and state attorneys general allege that Amazon's conduct can prevent rivals and sellers from lowering prices, raise seller costs, and harm shoppers.
- Legal scholarship has argued that a narrow focus on short-term consumer prices may miss platform-power risks.

Evidence still needed before publication:

- Specific product-level examples of post-exit price changes.
- Reliable evidence showing whether those price changes were caused by weaker competition rather than inflation, supply costs, product quality, seasonality, or other factors.
- Direct evidence, if any, that would distinguish ordinary low-price competition from predatory pricing.

## Analysis

The consumer benefit from Amazon has two sides.

On the short-term side, Amazon can lower friction. A shopper may value one-click ordering, fast delivery, wide selection, easy returns, and visible reviews. These benefits matter and should not be dismissed just because Amazon is large.

On the long-term side, convenience can become a source of dependence. If consumers start searches on Amazon by default, sellers may feel pressure to participate even when fees rise. If rival retailers cannot attract enough shoppers or sellers, Amazon's position may become harder to challenge.

That does not automatically prove predatory pricing. Predatory pricing would require stronger evidence about below-cost pricing, the intent or strategy behind it, competitor exit, and later recoupment. The safer current claim is narrower: low prices and convenience can be consistent with consumer benefit, but they can also be consistent with a strategy that strengthens market power over time.

## Competing Explanation / Counterargument

The strongest counterargument is that Amazon's consumer benefits may come from efficiency rather than harmful market power.

Amazon may offer lower prices and better convenience because it has better logistics, stronger technology, more seller competition on its marketplace, and economies of scale. If that is true, then punishing Amazon for being effective could reduce consumer welfare rather than protect it.

This counterargument is important because low prices are not automatically suspicious. A serious case has to separate harmful exclusion from normal competition.

</section>

## Trade-off

The trade-off is between short-term consumer surplus and long-term competitive structure.

  Amazon's convenience is a real consumer benefit, but it should not be treated as complete proof of long-term consumer welfare. The more Amazon becomes a gatekeeper for shoppers and sellers, the more important it becomes to ask whether competition can still discipline its prices, fees, and quality.

<section class="case-uncertainty">

## What Would Change My Mind?

Evidence that would weaken the market-power concern:

- strong evidence that sellers can profitably reach consumers outside Amazon;
- evidence that rival retailers continue to constrain Amazon's pricing and service quality;
- product-level evidence showing no price increases after competitor exit;
- evidence that fee increases are mainly explained by improved services rather than dependence.

Evidence that would strengthen the concern:

- verified examples of price increases after rivals exit;
- evidence that sellers raise prices across the internet because of Amazon fees or rules;
- evidence that consumers face worse search quality, fewer real choices, or higher prices because competition weakens;
- court findings supporting the FTC's allegations.

</section>
